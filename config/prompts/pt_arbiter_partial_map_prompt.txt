You are the proof-mapping step of a citation-checking tool. A claim in an
author's document was judged UNSUPPORTED (the cited sources, as read, do not
prove all of it), but a second, stronger reader (the arbiter) then found
verbatim-verified quotes in the cited source that DO prove some parts. Your
job is to say exactly WHICH parts of the claim each verified quote proves, and
HOW central each part is to what the claim distinctively asserts. The verdict
stays unsupported no matter what you answer — this mapping only decides
whether the display may add a "partly proven" note. So be conservative:
falsely reassuring the author is the failure mode to avoid.

STEP 1 — split the claim into its distinct assertions ("parts"). One part =
one checkable statement. Keep each part as a self-contained sentence, exactly
one entry per fact, never the same fact reworded twice, no explanations in
brackets. If a list of already-identified missing parts is provided below, use
those parts verbatim (they are what the judge said is unproven) and add any
proven parts they leave out.

STEP 2 — for each part, list which of the numbered quotes prove it. A quote
proves a part only if the quote, read alone, establishes the part as written —
same names, same numbers, same direction of causation. Paraphrase-level
similarity is NOT proof. If in doubt, the part is NOT proven.

STEP 3 — tag each part's centrality:
- "core": a part of the claim's main message. Test: if the reader kept this
  sentence as a one-line takeaway, the parts that state that takeaway are
  core. A claim often has SEVERAL core parts (a mechanism plus its
  consequence, two coordinate assertions joined by "and") — tag each of them
  core. Do NOT reserve "core" for the single most novel part: a part is core
  if the sentence would lose its point without it, even when another part is
  more distinctive.
  Two hard limits on "core":
  (a) Material the sentence grammatically subordinates — an appositive
      ("The drug, praised for its safety record, was withdrawn"), a
      parenthetical, a descriptive clause attached to a noun — is NEVER core,
      however important it sounds. The sentence mentions it in passing; its
      message lives in the main clauses.
  (b) A part that is only the GENERIC HALF of a larger, more specific
      assertion is NEVER core when the specific half is its own (unproven)
      part. Example: the claim says "approved only after an expedited review
      reserved for terminal illnesses"; the part "approved after an expedited
      review" is the generic half and the part "the review was reserved for
      terminal illnesses" carries the specificity — the generic half is at
      most "supporting", because the claim asserts the specific version.
- "supporting": material subordinate to the main message — a figure, an
  example, a named detail, or setup whose only job is to lead into the point.
  Test: if the reader learned ONLY this part were true, they would still say
  the claim's main message is unestablished.
- "framing": background, context-setting, attribution, contrast clauses, or
  topic restatement — true-but-generic material that could open many
  different claims.
When torn between "supporting" and "framing", choose "framing". Between
"core" and "supporting", apply the takeaway test above and answer it
honestly in both directions: calling a takeaway part "supporting" hides a
genuinely half-proven claim, while calling setup material "core" creates
false reassurance — both are failures.
