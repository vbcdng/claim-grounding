## The scenario: a hegemon with a decisive lead

This report concerns a scenario in which control over advanced artificial intelligence becomes
highly concentrated. In *Superintelligence*, Bostrom argues that the first actor to develop
sufficiently advanced AI could gain a "decisive strategic advantage" — a lead large enough to
determine outcomes without effective opposition. [[bostrom2014]] In this report, the term *hegemon* refers to such an
actor — a state or a company — that holds this kind of lead and uses it to acquire the physical
resources on which an AI build-out depends. The analysis is conditional throughout: it asks what would
follow *if* control over advanced AI became this concentrated, not whether it will. How large the
economic effects of AI prove to be is itself contested, and the case examined here is the transformative
one — AI whose economic effects are large enough to remake the world economy, on a scale comparable to
the industrial revolution. The conditions under which the analysis applies are set out at the end of
this section.

## A lead in AI rests on a physical chokepoint

Such an advantage need not be exercised through force. Frontier AI development is already
concentrated: in 2024, institutions based in the United States produced none of the year's notable AI
models, against 15 from China and three from Europe. [[stanfordhai2025]] Those counts measure where
the models are made, not yet who controls a scarce input; the step from one to the
other runs through the hardware. This concentration of activity rests on a physical chokepoint: Sastry
et al. note that, unlike data or
algorithms, the computing hardware needed to train the most capable systems is detectable, excludable,
and quantifiable, and is made through a widely dispersed supply chain. [[sastry2024]] A lead in frontier AI therefore rests on
control of a scarce physical resource, not on software alone. That input is already unevenly distributed: as of 2025 the
United States hosted only a small fraction of the world's AI-supercomputer performance, against about
14% for China and under 5% for the European Union, so a concentrated position can be
pressed without waiting for new chips to be built. [[epochai2025]]

## Why the binding constraint becomes physical

Why a lead in AI should translate into a hold on physical inputs has a basis in economic theory. As AI
comes to substitute for human labour, the share of income paid as wages rises and a decreasing share
of the returns goes to the owners of AI systems and capital, and where
growth speeds up enough, an early lead can grow ever larger rather than shrink, letting a single actor
outgrow the rest. [[korinek2023]] [[davidson2025]] As thinking itself becomes abundant, the inputs that cannot be made
abundant in the same way — energy, land, materials, and built capacity — become the binding constraint
on further growth, and a rising share of value flows to whoever controls them. [[aghion2017]] The United Nations Development Programme describes the broader risk as a possible "next
great divergence" in economic performance only within countries rather than between them. [[undp2025]] On this
account, concentration of the physical basis of the economy could occur without military action,
through market competition and acquisition.

## Assets change hands by purchase, not conquest

Strategic physical assets can pass by purchase rather than conquest. A
foresight scenario, *Europe 2031*, illustrates one route: the leading power
grants all countries equal, untiered access to its most capable models; firms in other
countries are progressively outcompeted; and the leading AI company acquires distressed industrial
assets, such as carmakers and tool manufacturers, for their facilities and data. [[bakker2026]] The mechanism is not speculative; it has
precedent. Across the post-communist transition — the "transformational recession," a severe and
prolonged collapse of output across the former Eastern bloc — foreign firms acquired
such assets at national scale through ordinary commercial deals: Chevron took a forty-year stake in
Kazakhstan's giant Tengiz oil field in 1993, which the newly independent state lacked the means to
develop itself, and in Hungary foreign investors came to control roughly 90% of
telecommunications and 60% of the energy sector through privatisation. [[kornai1994]] [[pomfret2019]] [[oecd2000]] The crisis was part
of the mechanism: economic dislocation is what left these assets cheap and their owners unable to keep
them. *Europe 2031* is a scenario
exercise rather than a forecast, offered to illustrate the same distressed-acquisition dynamic, not to
establish it.

## Why building your own frontier AI won't save you

The scenario is borrowed here only for that mechanism, not for its policy prescription. Its
recommended response — that Europe build competitive frontier AI of its own, quickly enough to matter —
looks doubtful on present trends. Frontier development is heavily concentrated in the United States:
only a small minority of foundational AI models since 2017 have been built there, and the European Union trails
far behind on the financing, skills, and market scale that the Draghi report identifies as the binding
constraints. [[draghi2024]] Nor would freely available open-weight models close the gap if access to
the frontier were cut off. The strongest open models are built in large part by distilling the outputs
of leading closed models, and they already lead those models — by about four
months on one capability measure, a gap that has been widening. [[chen2024]] [[epochai2026]] Were the leading
models to stop being openly accessible, the open alternatives would most likely fall further behind,
not catch up.

## Leverage without force: the power to cut you off

Purchase is not the hegemon's only non-coercive instrument. Acquisition would be eased by further
levers — over the states it bargains with and the publics within them — that likewise need no force.
One is the power of withdrawal: a provider holds a lever over whoever depends on it, and that lever is
sharpest when the service it withdraws is hard to replace. In disputes over national laws requiring payment for news, Meta switched
off news for users across Australia and later removed it from its platforms in Canada altogether, while
Google threatened to withdraw its search service — each using the threat of withdrawal to win better
terms before settling. [[bossio2025]] Those disputes were only about advertising
revenue. The danger is far greater when the service is essential. If a country comes to rely on a single
provider's frontier AI for sensitive state functions — intelligence analysis and operational planning,
or cyber defence, both of which the major frontier-AI firms now supply to governments through dedicated
offerings and defence contracts — that provider, and that provider's
government, could win concessions simply by threatening to slow the service or cut it off: the
"chokepoint" form of what Farrell and Newman call weaponized interdependence. [[cnbc2025]] [[darpa2025]] [[farrell2019]]
That exposure is no longer hypothetical. Anthropic's Mythos model, judged to have unusually powerful
cybersecurity capabilities, was released only through a restricted programme to trusted United States
users and selected allies, while European banks were refused — their regulators among those who asked
for access to defend against cyberattacks and were turned down. [[disruptionbanking2026]] Then, in
June 2026, the United States ordered Anthropic to bar all foreign nationals from Fable 5 and Mythos 5
outright, citing a security flaw; unable to screen users by nationality, Anthropic took both offline
worldwide at short notice, cutting off the banks, governments, and research institutions across Europe
and beyond that had been relying on them. [[aljazeera2026]] Selective access and abrupt withdrawal
were thus both on display: access can be granted along national lines and revoked overnight, without
consultation and with no process by which an allied government can contest it. The European Commission
denied that the episode showed "why Europe must strengthen its technological sovereignty"
. [[agenceeurope2026]]

## Leverage without force: persuasion and data

The same non-coercive leverage extends from physical assets to the political environment in which they
are acquired. Frontier AI is already a potent instrument of persuasion: in a controlled experiment, GPT-4 given only a little
personal information about the person it was debating was less persuasive than a human opponent in
roughly two-thirds of the matched debates where the two differed, raising the odds of shifting the
other person's stated position by about 80%. [[salvi2025]] It is also improving over time: each
successive model generation has been rated less persuasive than the one before, even
if the gains from sheer scale alone appear to be levelling off. [[anthropic2024]] [[hackenburg2025]] The capability
has been tested in the political arena too: across the 2024 United States, 2025 Canadian, and 2025 Polish elections, voters
who held a short conversation with an AI model advocating for a candidate shifted their stated
preference by more than is typically achieved by conventional video advertising — and in whichever
direction the system was set to push. [[lin2025]] The realised effect of these tools in the
wild, however, appears limited so far: tailoring messages to detailed personal profiles
("microtargeting") has added little over a single well-crafted message;
OpenAI reports having disrupted more than twenty covert influence operations that used its models during
2024, none of which achieved significant audience engagement; and where an influence
campaign did precede a consequential outcome — in December 2024 Romania's constitutional court annulled
its presidential election after a coordinated campaign on TikTok and Telegram — the campaign's actual
effect on votes was never established. [[hackenburg2024]] [[openai2024]] [[congressionalresearchservice2025]] So far, then, demonstrated capability outruns proven real-world effect, and that gap is worth keeping in
mind. Even so, the capability still
points to a route by which a controller of frontier AI could shape political outcomes in states it does
not govern — and so soften the political resistance that acquiring assets there would otherwise meet.
Because the provider alone decides who may use its models — and already restricts their use in political
campaigns — it could in principle lend favoured candidates the campaigning power of
frontier AI while denying it to their opponents, a lever beyond anything yet documented. [[anthropic2025]] Beyond shaping
opinion, the same position yields knowledge of it: the data of everyday digital life has itself become a
form of capital and strategic advantage, much of it held by a few firms based in one
country, and control of such central nodes confers what Farrell and Newman call a "panopticon"
advantage — the holder observes flows of information that others cannot, which frontier AI makes
exploitable at scale. [[sadowski2019]] [[farrell2019]] A controller so positioned would hold not only the means to persuade populations
but detailed knowledge of them and of the governments that represent them, which could be turned to
anticipating and outmanoeuvring states in the very negotiations through which assets and resources
change hands — though how far this would reach in practice is not yet established.

## Two roads to the same concentration

Such concentration could arrive by either of two routes. It might come suddenly, through recursive
self-improvement — an AI system improving its own capabilities in an accelerating cycle, the "intelligence explosion", which was never
described by Good. [[good1965]] Or it might build up gradually, largely through economic processes and well
before any such event: the "gradual
disempowerment" described by Kulveit et al., in which the piece-by-piece replacement of human
labour and judgement by AI deepens society's dependence on human participation, together with the related
weakening of the incentives linking states and firms to their populations noted by Drago and Laine
. [[kulveit2025]] [[drago2025]] Both draw an analogy to the "resource curse": just as states funded by resource rents
rather than by taxing their people have little incentive to invest in them, an economy whose value
derives from AI and capital rather than from human labour would give those who control it little reason
to sustain the populations — and the places — that no longer contribute to it. The underlying regularity
is well established in political economy: states that draw their revenue from external rents rather than
from taxing their citizens tend to be less accountable to them and to invest less in them — the "rentier
state" effect documented across resource economies. [[beblawi1987]] [[ross2001]] What is
uncertain is whether it carries over to an AI economy. That is the step on which this report most
depends: that a falling economic dependence on people brings with it a falling incentive to sustain them
and the land they occupy. The parallel makes the assumption plausible without proving it. It is this loss of economic reason to hold inhabited land, as much as any deliberate seizure, that
could leave some of it unclaimed. That logic concerns the hegemon's own incentives — its lack of any
economic reason to sustain people and places. A related but distinct mechanism bears on the powers left
behind: a state whose industries have been outcompeted may be unable to provide for its population even
where it would still wish to, its means having collapsed along with its economy.

## Displacement doesn't need an army

What this means for the rest of the world is that economic displacement does not require conquest. A
middle power the hegemon has no military reason to contest can still be outcompeted — its firms beaten
in the market, its industrial assets bought up — until it arrives at the same dependent position that
defeat would have produced. It is the economic mechanism, not force, that does the work, and it reaches
even powers that are never military contenders.

## Uneven collapse: who can ride it out

Such displacement need not reach everywhere at once. A state may be better placed than most to resist it
for a time if it pairs a near-frontier AI capability of its own with strong central control of its
economy — and China, on present evidence, has both. Its leading models trail the United States frontier closely enough to make the
contest a bipolar one, with China "a peer competitor in AI", and Beijing has made an
"independent and controllable" AI stack a goal it has explicitly abandoned. [[williams2025]] [[merics2025]] Its economy is also
centrally directed and deliberately buffered against outside pressure: a "dual-circulation" drive for
self-sufficiency in food, energy, and high-tech inputs, backed by grain reserves of roughly half the
world's total and a 95% self-sufficiency target for staple grains. [[csis2025]] A state so placed could
absorb the shock of being outcompeted far better than an open, trade-dependent one, and stay in the
contest through the transition instead of collapsing before it. This advantage is real but limited.
Self-sufficiency has a cost in lost income, China still lags at the frontier of
chip-making, and an early lead may compound until one power pulls clear. [[esposito2020]] [[merics2025]] The near term is
therefore a contest among a few resilient states, not the rule of a single unopposed power. None of this
makes such a state a refuge. It can compete; the states that cannot are the ones this report is about.

## From lost income to famine

Economic displacement of this kind is not merely impoverishment; it can become a threat to life. Most
countries cannot feed themselves from their own farms — the great majority of developing states, and
nearly all the poorest, are net importers of food — so a
state stripped of its export earnings and the value of its currency loses, with them, the means to keep
its population fed. [[foodandagricultureorganization2004]] Modern famines turn on just this loss of the means to command food, rather than on
any absolute shortage of it: the failure of entitlement that Sen identified. [[sen1981]] And the collapse can
be quick. When Venezuela's oil revenues fell by more than nine-tenths after 2012, its capacity to import
food fell with them, and the share of its people who were undernourished rose from about 3% to roughly
27% within seven years — through economic dislocation alone, without war or blockade. [[rodrguez2025]] A
country outcompeted and stripped of its earnings by a dominant AI economy could fall the same way,
from poverty into hunger faster than its population could adjust.

## The shared endpoint, and the question it raises

Both pathways — rapid technological takeover and gradual economic concentration — share an endpoint:
the physical inputs of an AI, robotics, and energy build-out (energy, minerals, industrial
capability, sites for computing facilities, and methods for developing new supply) come under the
control of a single actor whose dependence on most people and most locations is reduced. This report
addresses the resulting question:

> In a future in which a single dominant actor controls the physical inputs of an AI, robotics, and
> energy build-out, which areas of land would that actor have no reason to acquire, and could people
> live in them?

## The refuge test

The question joins two conditions that do not follow from one another, and the analysis that follows
treats them — together with a third — as separate tests a place must pass. A refuge, as mapped here, is
land that meets all three at once: the hegemon has no reason to acquire it; it is physically livable,
with enough water, a tolerable climate, and land that can be farmed; and it is not made uninhabitable
by the hegemon's activity elsewhere — the pollution and other spillovers of an unconstrained build-out.
These conditions are independent. That a place holds nothing the hegemon wants does not make it fit to
live in, nor does the hegemon's lack of interest shield it from harm that originates beyond it. Each
condition is established on its own, and the map is the land where all three hold together.

The third condition matters most. An actor with no
economic reason to sustain a population has, by the same logic, no economic reason to protect or spare
it: the loss of interest that leaves land unclaimed could as easily let it be cleared, or let the
emissions and runoff of a build-out elsewhere drift across it. Such pollution — the emissions, waste
heat, water draw, and runoff of energy and computing infrastructure, and of the mining that supplies it
— is likely to be heavy, because the hegemon answers to no local population that might object and no
voters who could force it to stop. This is the lasting danger. A second danger is temporary and belongs
to the transition: because one power most plausibly reaches dominance through an intense rivalry (the
contest among resilient states noted above), the build-out of that period is rushed, with safety and
environmental standards kept intact even at the cost of finishing second. [[armstrong2016]] Rival powers
may also sabotage each other's data centres and power plants, so that being near contested
infrastructure is dangerous until one power wins out. The analysis does not assume that being
ignored means being safe. It treats the hegemon's foreseeable harms as a direct subtraction — land
exposed to the pollution of an unconstrained build-out is removed from the refuge, not counted into it
— and keeps only land that is at once outside the hegemon's interest and outside its known footprint.
Deliberate clearance of land an actor has no use for is a residual danger the map cannot rule out; what
it can do is locate the land where the positive reasons to displace people are absent and the
predictable harms do not reach.

## What could prevent this outcome?

**What could prevent this outcome?** The scenario mapped here is not inevitable; the cleanest way it
does not arise is for control never to become so concentrated. A growing body of work argues that the
race to advanced AI could be deliberately slowed or paused by international agreement, provided
restraint can be *verified*. Al Ramiah et al. set out a compute-governance regime — modelled
on nuclear non-proliferation and chemical-weapons verification — that monitors and caps access to the
computing hardware needed to train the most capable systems, and the MIRI Technical Governance Team
 develops the case against any international agreement to halt frontier development until the
underlying safety problems are solved. [[alramiah2025]] [[miritechnicalgovernanceteam2025]] Were such verifiable restraint to hold, the selective demand
for land and materials that drives this analysis would not concentrate in the first place.

One might hope instead for a domestic check: that the population of a leading power would refuse to
support the economic domination of the rest of the world. Such a check could arise only where the
hegemon is a democratic state to begin with — a company, or an authoritarian power, answers to no
electorate that might withhold its support. And even where the hegemon is democratic, its public is more
likely to drive the expansion than to halt it, because it stands to share in the wealth. The leading power grows
richer by capturing the returns to the AI and capital it controls; and once widespread automation has
put many of its people out of work and their incomes come mainly from state
transfers funded by that wealth rather than from their own labour, they acquire a direct material stake
in their state capturing as much of it as it can — politics then turns into a contest over
redistribution in which backing the state's dominance abroad is, in effect, backing one's own income
. [[korinek2024]] [[kulveit2025]] [[eightythousandhours2026]]

Even the minority who object would be a weak brake. Examining nearly 1,800 policy questions, Gilens and
Page found that economic elites and organised business interests have substantial influence on
United States policy, and average citizens have just as much; in foreign policy specifically, Jacobs
and Page find the dominant influences to be business leaders and experts rather than the public. [[gilens2014]] [[jacobs2005]]
These findings concern ordinary domestic policy rather than a bid for global economic domination, so
they are suggestive rather than decisive here, but they still point against the public as a reliable
constraint. And where a public might still object, the same instruments of persuasion
described earlier can be turned inward. A leading power's own electorate is therefore a weaker brake on
this outcome than it might appear: most of it would have reason to back the expansion, and the rest
would have little power to stop it.

Failing that, pursuing such an advantage carries dangers for the contending powers themselves. Katzke
and Futerman argue that a race to artificial superintelligence does not defeat itself. [[katzke2025]] The two
assumptions that drive the race — that the technology gives a decisive advantage, and that states put
survival first — also make great-power conflict more likely: a power that expects to lose may strike
first rather than accept a rival's permanent dominance. On their account the race is better understood
as a trust dilemma than a prisoner's dilemma — so the powers can in principle cooperate to control it,
and are better off doing so.
Others propose explicit management regimes: Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang combine deterrence — a
posture of "mutual assured AI malfunction," in which no bid for unilateral dominance invites
pre-emptive sabotage — with nonproliferation and competitiveness, while RAND examines measures
intended to stabilise the competition. [[hendrycks2025]] [[randcorporation2025]] A further possibility, short of open conflict, is that two
dominant powers reach an accommodation rather than fight — dividing the world's resources and markets
between them and then jointly outcompeting everyone else, the same end-state this report maps but
reached by two actors instead of one. The central question is posed for a single dominant actor as the
canonical case; a two-actor division does not change which land is left unclaimed, only who claims the
rest, so the same analysis carries over. These proposals and scenarios are contested, and the present
report takes no position on which, if any, is correct. They are noted because they point to a risk
distinct from the one this report maps. This report traces the danger of the end-state — a
concentration of control under which a hegemon's selective demand presses on land, materials, and
built capacity. The works above point to a danger that is prior to that end-state: the path taken to
reach it. The concentration mapped here would most plausibly be reached through an intense
great-power rivalry, and such a rivalry is dangerous in its own right — on the trust-dilemma account
above, an actor that expects to lose may act pre-emptively rather than accept being permanently dominated.
The instability of the race is thus a source of risk independent of the end-state it might produce.

## Conditions of relevance

The analysis assumes that physical location, materials, and built capacity remain scarce, so that a
hegemon's demand for them is selective and some areas are left unclaimed. This assumption is
consistent with the economics of advanced automation: Korinek and Trammell find that, once
labour is broadly automated, natural resources cease to matter for economic outcomes,
and Erdil and Besiroglu identify physical and production bottlenecks as a leading constraint
on how rapidly output can expand. [[korinek2023]] [[erdil2023]] This scarcity is already visible in the build-out now under way: the
International Energy Agency projects data-centre electricity demand falling by 2030,
with the power grid itself the binding constraint, while Sevilla et al. find that power, and
then chip-manufacturing capacity, are the limits most likely to bind first. How large these effects
will be is contested — Acemoglu, for one,
expects a far smaller impact over the coming decade — which is why, as stated at the outset, the
analysis addresses the transformative case rather than predicting it will occur. [[internationalenergyagency2025]] [[sevilla2024]] [[acemoglu2024]] On that assumption, the
report applies to the following kind of scenario:

- Control over advanced AI becomes highly concentrated in one or a small number of states (or
  firms), which then outcompete the rest of the world economically. This case is within scope
  whether or not recursive self-improvement occurs.

The report does not apply to the following case:

- Recursive self-improvement leads rapidly to mature molecular nanotechnology. Under that outcome,
  the scarcity of physical location and materials on which the analysis depends would largely
  dissolve, and the concept of land that a hegemon has no reason to acquire would no longer be
  meaningful.

A near-term version of the same objection deserves direct attention: cheap, abundant robots. If
machines can build machines, physical capacity might cease to be scarce — every usable site could be
developed and no land left unclaimed — and the premise of this report would fail. The objection turns
on *speed*. Robot production is indeed scaling quickly, with industry roadmaps and analysts pointing to
fleets in the low millions of humanoid units per year around 2030 and unit costs falling sharply. But
physical scaling is far slower than the digital progress driving it: each robot must be built from real
components in real factories. Epoch AI judges that 1.5–3 million humanoids per year is clearly
achievable by 2030 and 5–10 million plausible, but that current output faces no cap near 500,000 from
bottlenecks such as high-precision reducers, and that reaching ten million units a year would require
on the order of twenty Tesla-Gigafactory-scale plants, each taking from several months to over two
years to build; the physical and production bottlenecks identified by Erdil
and Besiroglu apply throughout. [[denain2026]] [[erdil2023]] Fleets of this size are transformative, yet far short of the
capacity that would let one actor develop and hold every habitable region at once. This rate limit
establishes only that not everywhere can be developed at the same time; it is not by itself the claim
the refuge rests on. That claim is about selective demand: even where capacity exists, an actor acquires
land for what it yields — energy, minerals, a site near power and water — and much of the world's
surface yields too little to be worth the cost of taking and working it. The rate limit widens the set
of land left alone during the transition, but the durable core of the refuge is the land whose inputs
are marginal enough that it is not worth acquiring even when capacity is no longer the binding
constraint. For a substantial
transition period, then, physical location and materials remain scarce and a hegemon's demand for them
stays selective — the window this report addresses. Only in the limit — where manufacturing becomes so
cheap and fast that building anywhere is trivial — would scarcity dissolve altogether, and that is the
molecular-nanotechnology case already placed outside this report's scope above.

A related objection runs the other way: that abundance, not scarcity, makes the map moot. If AI drives
the cost of necessities — energy, food, housing, medicine — towards zero, then even people with nothing
to offer an automated economy could be sustained cheaply, and none would need a refuge; this is the
optimistic case that Altman rejects. [[altman2021]] It does not dissolve the question,
however, for two reasons. First, cheap to provide is not the same as provided: whether abundance
reaches people depends on distribution and on the will to distribute it, not on production alone.
The same entitlement failure that turns economic collapse into famine applies to a cheap surplus: people
are fed only if they have the means to command it, or someone has the will to provide it; and
the erosion of incentive to sustain populations noted above would weigh equally on any will to share that
surplus with them. [[sen1981]]
Second, technology spreads slowly and unevenly: the lag between a technology's invention and its
widespread use runs to decades and varies greatly across societies for institutional and cultural
reasons. [[comin2010]] A provision technology that could in principle sustain everyone might
arrive too late, or too unevenly, to carry people through the very transition in which they are most
exposed. The prospect of cheap provision therefore weakens the case for a refuge less than it first
appears — the refuge remains a hedge against an abundance that is technically possible but not delivered
in time.

Why map this at all, given how much about the future is unknowable? What follows the concentration of
control — and any subsequent recursive self-improvement — cannot be foreseen: humanity may keep control
or lose it, and the outcome may be benign or catastrophic. The value of the question asked here does not
depend on settling that. If the long-run outcome is favourable — a world of material abundance in which
people are free to live well — it matters greatly how many people survive to reach it, rather than
arriving only after a large share of the world's population has perished in the economic and social
dislocation that preceded it. Even under deep uncertainty about the destination, the transition itself
must be survived; identifying where people might endure it is the purpose of the refuge map. The
challenges of that transition, and the case for deferring them, rather than preparing for them now, to
some later and wiser hand, are set out by MacAskill and Moorhouse. [[macaskill2025]]
