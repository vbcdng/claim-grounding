Why Superforecasters Struggle With AI — and Not With Everything Else

Superforecasters earned their reputation in domains built on recurring structure: elections, GDP prints, conflict escalation, disease spread. Geopolitical forecasting is, on its face, an "algorithm-unfriendly" domain — hard-to-quantify inputs, elusive reference classes — yet trained human forecasters, especially when aggregated, hold their own against statistical models there, because research comparing clinical and statistical prediction has shown that algorithms predict more accurately than humans in many domains, but geopolitical forecasting is an algorithm-unfriendly domain, with hard-to-quantify data and elusive reference classes that make predictive model-building difficult  [[mellers2023]]. AI progress denies forecasters exactly that kind of scaffolding, and the tournament record shows it starkly.

In the 2022 Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament, benchmarks resolving by 2024–2025 were forecast by 169 superforecasters and domain experts  [[karger2023]] [[kucinskas2025]]. Respondents, especially superforecasters, underestimated AI progress — for three standard AI benchmarks (MATH, MMLU, and QuALITY), domain experts gave probabilities of 21.4%, 25.0%, and 43.5% respectively to outcomes achieved by end of 2024, while superforecasters were even more pessimistic at 9.3%, 7.2%, and 20.1%  [[kucinskas2025]]. AI systems reached a gold-level Olympiad performance to which domain experts gave only 8.6% and superforecasters a mere 2.3% probability; across four AI benchmarks, superforecasters overall gave observed outcomes an average probability of just 9.7%, versus 24.6% from domain experts  [[kucinskas2025]]. At the same time, the same cohort overestimated improvements in climate technology — an error in the opposite direction  [[kucinskas2025]].

Independent evidence points the same way. Steinhardt's parallel exercise found that the AI experts had similar medians but were overconfident in the tails, whereas the superforecasters systematically predicted too little progress, and generalist crowd forecasters did worst  [[steinhardt2023]]. FRI's ongoing expert panel shows this continuing into 2025–2026: recent benchmark performance suggests that the median expert substantially underestimates the progress of development of SOTA AI systems  [[fri2026]].

The structural problem is reference-class reasoning. Superforecasting leans on outside-view base rates from many similar past events; elections and wars recur, GDP mean-reverts. By contrast, AI capability growth is compute-driven and discontinuous, so there is no comparable historical baseline  [[gruetzemacher2021]]. Tellingly, the near-term performance of individual forecasters could not be statistically distinguished from simple algorithms — a "no-change" forecast and trend extrapolation — and near-term accuracy had no statistically significant correlation with long-term existential-risk forecasts  [[kucinskas2025]].

The disagreement runs deeper than calibration. In an adversarial-collaboration study, "concerned" domain experts put the chance of AI-caused existential catastrophe by 2100 at 20%, while "skeptical" participants — mainly superforecasters — put it at 0.12%, and neither side substantially updated toward the other's views  [[rosenberg2024]]. One reading is that epistemic uncertainty (unknown mechanism), not aleatoric uncertainty (a stable random process), dominates existential AI risk — exactly the kind of ignorance that standard probability elicitation, the superforecaster's core tool, was not built to represent  [[lohn2026]].

Geopolitics rewards forecasters skilled at averaging history. AI progress currently rewards betting that history's pace won't hold — which is precisely the bet superforecasters, drilled to distrust extrapolation, are least equipped to make.
